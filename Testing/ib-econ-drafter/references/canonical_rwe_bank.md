# Canonical Real-World Examples (RWE) Bank for IB Economics

To achieve Level 7 (13–15 marks) on Paper 1 Part (b) essays, real-world examples must not be generic or hypothetical ("e.g., country X"). They must be **specific, named, dated, and accompanied by economic context and empirical outcomes**.

## 1. Unit 2: Microeconomics

### 1.1 Indirect Taxes (Correcting Demerit Goods & Negative Externalities of Consumption)
* **Example 1: United Kingdom Soft Drinks Industry Levy (SDIL, 2018)**
  * **Policy Details**: Tiered sugar levy implemented in April 2018. Charged 18p/litre on drinks with 5g–8g sugar per 100ml, and 24p/litre on drinks with >8g sugar per 100ml.
  * **Mechanism**: Shifted supply curve vertically upwards by the tax amount, raising consumer price ($P_c$) and reducing quantity consumed ($Q$).
  * **Empirical Outcome / Evaluation**: Over 50% of manufacturers reformulated drinks before implementation to avoid the tax (reducing sugar content by 45 million kg per year). Demonstrates high producer responsiveness and reduction in negative externalities on public healthcare (NHS).
* **Example 2: Mexico Soda Tax (IEPS, 2014)**
  * **Policy Details**: 1 peso per litre excise tax on sugar-sweetened beverages.
  * **Empirical Outcome**: Purchases of taxed beverages fell by 5.5% in Year 1 and 9.7% in Year 2. Disproportionate burden fell on lower-income households (regressive tax incidence).

### 1.2 Subsidies & Merit Goods (Positive Externalities of Consumption/Production)
* **Example 1: United States Inflation Reduction Act (IRA, 2022) — Clean Vehicle Credits**
  * **Policy Details**: Up to $7,500 tax credit/subsidy for consumers purchasing qualifying new electric vehicles (EVs) assembled in North America.
  * **Mechanism**: Lowers effective price to consumers ($P_c$), shifting demand rightward/supply downward toward socially optimal quantity ($Q^*$).
  * **Evaluation**: Accelerates decarbonization and economies of scale in battery production, but creates substantial opportunity cost ($369B green energy package) and trade friction with EU/Asian automakers over domestic content rules.
* **Example 2: Singapore Medisave / Government Subsidies on Preventive Healthcare**
  * **Policy Details**: Up to 80% direct subsidy at public polyclinics for essential vaccines (e.g. HPV, childhood immunizations).
  * **Outcome**: Near 95% coverage rate, eliminating underconsumption due to positive health externalities.

### 1.3 Price Controls (Ceilings and Floors)
* **Price Ceiling Example: Berlin Rent Cap (*Mietendeckel*, 2020–2021)**
  * **Policy Details**: State of Berlin froze rents for 1.5 million apartments at 2019 levels and set a strict ceiling between €6 and €10 per square meter.
  * **Mechanism**: $P_{\max}$ set below free-market equilibrium rent, creating immediate shortage ($Q_d > Q_s$).
  * **Empirical Outcome**: Available rental listings collapsed by over 50% as landlords converted apartments to private sales or left them vacant; black-market "side contracts" emerged for furniture/renovation. Declared unconstitutional in April 2021.
* **Price Floor Example: United Kingdom National Living Wage (NLW, 2024)**
  * **Policy Details**: Mandatory minimum wage raised to £11.44 per hour for workers aged 21 and over.
  * **Evaluation**: Increases income for low-wage workers and reduces relative poverty; however, raises labor costs for SME employers in hospitality and retail, risking small-business insolvencies and substituting labor with automation.

### 1.4 Market-Based Environmental Policies (Negative Externalities of Production)
* **Example: European Union Emissions Trading System (EU ETS)**
  * **Policy Details**: World's largest cap-and-trade market, covering over 10,000 industrial installations and 40% of EU GHG emissions. Carbon price exceeded €80–€100/tonne in 2022–2023.
  * **Mechanism**: Sets strict declining cap on total emissions; polluters must hold EU Allowances (EUAs). Internalizes the external cost of carbon ($MPC \to MSC$).
  * **Evaluation**: Power and industrial sector emissions dropped by over 35% since 2005; however, risks "carbon leakage" (addressed via the new EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism - CBAM).

## 2. Unit 3: Macroeconomics

### 2.1 Fiscal Policy
* **Expansionary Fiscal Policy: United States American Rescue Plan (2021)**
  * **Policy Details**: $1.9 trillion stimulus package including $1,400 direct stimulus checks, expanded child tax credits, and $350B state/local government aid.
  * **Mechanism**: Direct injection into $C$ and $G$, shifting Aggregate Demand rightward ($AD_1 \to AD_2$) to close the COVID deflationary output gap.
  * **Evaluation**: Rapid recovery in GDP (+5.9% in 2021) and low unemployment (3.6%); but severely fueled demand-pull inflation (CPI reached 9.1% in June 2022) and pushed national debt above 120% of GDP.
* **Contractionary Fiscal Policy: United Kingdom Autumn Statement (2022)**
  * **Policy Details**: £55 billion fiscal consolidation (spending cuts + freezing income tax thresholds).
  * **Outcome**: Stabilized gilt bond markets after the mini-budget crisis, but exacerbated real wage contraction and stagnant growth.

### 2.2 Monetary Policy
* **Contractionary Monetary Policy: US Federal Reserve Rate Hike Cycle (2022–2023)**
  * **Policy Details**: Fed raised the Federal Funds Target Rate from 0.00%–0.25% in March 2022 to 5.25%–5.50% by July 2023.
  * **Mechanism**: Higher borrowing costs reduce consumer expenditure ($C$) on credit/housing and firm investment ($I$), shifting $AD$ left to tame demand-pull inflation.
  * **Evaluation**: Headline CPI fell from 9.1% (2022) to 3.0% (2024) without triggering a full recession ("soft landing"); however, increased mortgage rates to 7%+ and induced regional banking stress (e.g. Silicon Valley Bank collapse).

### 2.3 Supply-Side Policies
* **Interventionist: United States CHIPS and Science Act (2022)**
  * **Policy Details**: $52.7 billion in direct subsidies and research funding for domestic semiconductor fabrication (TSMC Arizona, Intel Ohio).
  * **Mechanism**: Increases capital stock and technological capability, shifting $LRAS$ to the right (increasing potential output $Y_p$).
  * **Evaluation**: Strengthens national economic security and creates high-skilled jobs; however, long gestation lag (takes 3–5 years to build foundries) and risk of government failure / wasteful subsidies.
* **Market-Based: UK Deregulation and Corporate Tax Competition**
  * **Details**: Establishment of 12 Freeports with zero stamp duty, capital allowances, and streamlined planning regulations to attract foreign direct investment.

## 3. Unit 4: The Global Economy

### 3.1 Protectionism & Trade Measures
* **Tariffs Example: US Section 301 Tariffs on Chinese Imports (2018–Present)**
  * **Policy Details**: 25% tariff on over $350 billion worth of Chinese industrial, electronics, and consumer imports, maintained and expanded under the Biden administration (e.g. 100% on Chinese EVs in 2024).
  * **Mechanism**: Increases domestic price from world price $P_w$ to $P_{w+t}$, shrinking import volumes from $(Q_4-Q_1)$ to $(Q_3-Q_2)$, generating tariff revenue and deadweight loss triangles ($DWL_1$ production inefficiency, $DWL_2$ consumption inefficiency).
  * **Evaluation**: Protected select domestic steel/manufacturing jobs, but led to retaliatory tariffs by China on US soybeans, raised component costs for US manufacturers, and reduced net real household income.

### 3.2 Exchange Rate Dynamics
* **Depreciation Example: Japanese Yen (JPY) Depreciation (2022–2024)**
  * **Context**: Bank of Japan (BOJ) maintained ultra-loose yield curve control while the US Fed raised rates to 5.5%, widening the interest rate differential.
  * **Mechanism**: Capital outflows shifted supply of Yen right and demand left, causing the Yen to plunge from ~115 JPY/USD in early 2022 to over 160 JPY/USD in mid-2024.
  * **Evaluation**: Surged record profits for Japanese multinational exporters (Toyota) and inbound tourism boom, but imported raw material and energy costs soared, generating severe domestic cost-push inflation.
